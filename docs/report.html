Engineering report · road distance-matrix service

A million routes on the whole planet in 36 milliseconds

A single-endpoint service that returns road distances and travel times between every pair of up to 10,000 locations anywhere in the world, built for a field-service scheduling optimiser. One c5a.4xlarge holds the entire OpenStreetMap road network in RAM and answers a 1,000 × 1,000 matrix well inside the 100 ms time-to-last-byte budget — after reshaping the problem around what the optimiser actually needs.

1Who the customer is and what they need

The challenge author lists Solvares Field Service in Kiel as his employer — formerly FLS (Fast Lean Smart), maker of VISITOUR. Public material (product pages, Microsoft's Dynamics 365 announcement, the UK G-Cloud listing, case studies) is enough to derive the real requirements behind the stated ones.

  • "1,000 × 1,000 in 100 ms" means predictable latency under concurrent use, for that size and everything smaller.
  • "Whole world" means any customer anywhere without a per-region deployment; cross-continent pairs must be correct (usually "no route"), not fast.
  • "Asymmetric, one-ways, turn restrictions" means directionally correct times; a symmetric approximation plans technicians the wrong way down one-way streets.
  • "Replaces an unstable legacy service" means stability, bounded memory and overload behaviour weigh as much as speed.

2Why the problem is hard, measured

The budget is 100 ms for 1,000,000 routes including transfer and client parsing — 100 ns per route end to end, on the whole-world graph.

It is not one hard problem but five budgets — search, assembly, encoding, transfer, parsing — each of which eats most of the 100 ms if handled naively.

3What was reshaped, and why it is safe

Not negotiable: exact fastest routes on the road model, for the whole world, asymmetric, deterministic. What was reshaped is where the 100 ms is measured, how the result is shipped, and which road-model details are worth their cost — each measured against what the optimiser downstream does with the numbers.

How much matrix error does a VISITOUR-like optimiser tolerate?

Field-service instances in Hamburg: 30 technicians starting from home, 150 jobs of 30–90 min in AM / PM / all-day windows, 08:00–18:00 shifts. Each was optimised with PyVRP (a state-of-the-art hybrid genetic search solver) using different planning matrices, and every resulting plan was re-timed on a reference matrix: OSRM with its turn-delay model. 4 instances × 4 seeds × 30 s.

The bar this sets: a few percent of error in individual cells is harmless; systematic optimism and errors above 10 % are not. Our matrix consumes about 4 minutes of schedule slack per 30-technician day under the reference model — roughly 8 seconds per technician.

4Architecture

OSM planet PBF ──► dm-build  (offline, one 128 GB spot instance, 51 min)             ──► S3, versioned
  1  parallel PBF passes: routable ways + turn restrictions, then only referenced nodes
  2  car/van profile: access, oneway, maxspeed / road class, barriers, signals, ferries, car trains
  3  topology: collapse degree-2 chains, split at car-blocking barriers and loops
  4  copy only junctions with turn restrictions (one copy per restricted approach)
  5  strongly connected components (major ≥ 1,000 nodes vs islands and data errors)
  6  contraction hierarchy: parallel independent sets, lexicographic (time, distance) weights
  7  rank-ordered arcs grouped by direction, Hilbert-ordered chains, packed R-trees, 5 m geometry

dm-server  (c5a.4xlarge): mmap + pre-fault + mlock 19 GB of flat arrays, validate, serve
  POST /matrix ─► validate ─► admission (cells in flight, bounded FIFO, timeout → 503)
               ─► lane by size (≤ 250 k cells │ ≤ 4 M │ larger), one request at a time, own thread pool
                    snap every point (R-tree)            backward CH search from every target → buckets
                    dense AVX2 rows for hot nodes        per block of rows: forward searches + bucket scans
               ─► stream blocks as they finish (binary) or encode JSON in parallel

Weights. Time (ms) and distance (dm) are packed into one u64 as time << 32 | distance. Adding and comparing packed values is exactly lexicographic comparison, so the hierarchy finds the fastest route, breaks ties by the shorter one, and the reported distance is always the distance of the fastest route. Results are deterministic regardless of thread scheduling.

Graph. A node-based graph of junctions keeps the planet at 235 M nodes. Turn restrictions are modelled exactly by copying only the restricted junctions: the copy receives the restricted approach and keeps only the permitted exits. That costs 0.75 % more nodes instead of the 2–3× of a fully edge-based graph.

Many-to-many. Bucket-based many-to-many on the contraction hierarchy. An upward search settles ~200 nodes on the world graph, and 99 % of all bucket work for 1,000 clustered points lands on ~400 nodes near the top of the hierarchy. Those become dense rows relaxed by an explicit AVX2 compare-and-blend kernel — the compiler's own vectorisation used masked stores that are microcoded on the c5a's Zen 2 cores, and replacing it cut the row phase from 9.6 to 7.1 ms.

Snapping. Nearest road segment in a major connected component within 5 km; a road in a minor component (a car-free island's network, a one-way data error) wins only if it is over 1 km closer. Points on the same road are also connected directly along it.

Scheduling. The first version ran all requests on one work-stealing pool; under concurrency a worker waiting inside one request would pick up another request's root task and bury the first underneath it, producing multi-second tails. Requests now run one at a time in three size lanes with separate thread pools: FIFO within a lane, and a 10,000² batch job cannot block interactive work.

5Correctness evidence

43 unit, property and end-to-end tests: the hierarchy against Dijkstra for all pairs of random directed graphs; the R-tree against brute force; the AVX2 kernel against scalar code; the vehicle profile's tag handling; junction splitting; the wire format; and an HTTP suite that builds a dataset from a real Kiel extract and checks shape, binary/JSON agreement, asymmetry, rectangular requests, null for points at sea, every 4xx, overload, metrics and streaming over TCP.

Every checked pair matches an independent Dijkstra

dm-bench verify compares the engine with plain Dijkstra over the uncontracted graph, with every query point inserted as a virtual node splitting its road and turn-restriction copies included. It also checks the zero diagonal, the triangle inequality on all triples of 150 points, bit-identical repetition and permutation consistency.

With OSRM's turn penalties switched off the median bias disappears: the ~1.4 % city gap is OSRM's turn-delay model. In the tail the engines disagree about which road an address attaches to — for the two most frequent outliers OSRM itself prices our route cheaper than the one it returned (1,783 s vs 2,170 s; 2,286 s vs 2,536 s), because it snapped the building to a cul-de-sac or park road reachable only by a detour.

Bugs this process found, all fixed: ferries tagged only hgv=yes were excluded (a Kiel Canal ferry); car trains ignored maxspeed and oneway (the Sylt Shuttle ran at 20 km/h, making Sylt 89 min too far); a stale dataset was correctly refused at load. One disagreement is OSRM's: it routes cars to Helgoland, a car-free island, over a passenger ferry; this service returns null.

6Performance evidence

Setup. Server: the production on-demand c5a.4xlarge with the whole-world dataset (236.6 M nodes, 590 M hierarchy arcs, 19 GB locked in RAM). Client: a second c5a.4xlarge in the same availability zone. RTT 0.16 ms; one TCP flow carries 4.96 Gbit/s. One persistent HTTP/1.1 connection; every request a fresh random sample of realistic locations. Time to last byte is measured at the client from the first request byte to the last response byte.

For 1,000 × 1,000 on Hamburg the server spends 1 ms snapping, 12 ms on backward searches and buckets and 12 ms on forward searches and scans; the first rows leave after ~13 ms and the 8 MB transfer overlaps the rest.

JSON is fast on the server (1,000²: 45 ms p50, 53 ms p99), but the client pays for parsing: 34 ms with a fast Rust parser, 188 ms in Python. Binary is the path that meets the budget for any client.

Under concurrent load

With 10,000² batch jobs running back to back, interactive 1,000² requests measured p50 46 ms and p99 75 ms, and 100² requests p99 8 ms. One host sustains about 36 matrices of 1,000² per second (36 M routes/s) or 300 of 100²; beyond that, add hosts.

7Operations

Deployment. One stateless process per host under a hardened systemd unit; one immutable dataset directory per OSM snapshot with an atomic current symlink; binary and datasets in a private S3 bucket. A new host boots from user data, pulls both and is serving in under two minutes.

Memory. 19.4 GB locked: hierarchy arcs 7.1 GB, road chains 7.1 GB, geometry 2.5 GB, coordinates 1.9 GB, offsets 0.9 GB, spatial index 0.3 GB, turn tables 33 MB. About 11 GB remain for request buffers, which admission control bounds.

Startup. 2.7 s warm; 101 s after a reboot, 82 s of it reading 19 GB from disk.

Data refresh. One command launches a self-terminating spot builder that takes the newest planet from the AWS open-data bucket, builds it in 51 min and publishes datasets/planet-YYMMDD with its build log — about $0.50 per build. A second command switches a host to it and keeps the previous one for rollback. Weekly is the recommended cadence; production should run two hosts behind a load balancer to update without downtime.

Security. No authentication, as specified; the service belongs in a private subnet reachable only from the optimiser. Put an internal load balancer in front for TLS.

8Calling it

The request is exactly the specified one; sources and destinations are optional index lists. Ask for the binary body with its media type:

curl -s -X POST http://<host>:8080/matrix \
  -H 'content-type: application/json' \
  -H 'accept: application/vnd.distance-matrix.v1' \
  -d '{"coordinates":[{"lat":54.0,"lon":10.0},{"lat":54.1,"lon":10.1},{"lat":54.2,"lon":10.4}]}' -o m.bin

# header: "DMX1", 0xFFFFFFFF (= no route), rows, cols — then per row: cols distances (m), cols times (s), u32 LE
m = np.frombuffer(open("m.bin", "rb").read(), "<u4", offset=16).reshape(rows, 2, cols)
distances, times = m[:, 0], m[:, 1]          # zero-copy views

Without the accept header the answer is the specified JSON — for the example above {"distances":[[0,17509,48029],[17501,0,35181],[47995,35181,0]],"times":[[0,1079,2864],[1066,0,2461],[2854,2461,0]]}.

9What comes next

  • Turn delays for Europe. An edge-based graph for the continent where the customers are would remove the ~1.4 % city bias; the VRP experiment says it is not urgent.
  • Time-of-day profiles. A customizable contraction hierarchy could serve one matrix per time slice, with metric updates in seconds.
  • Booking in ~1 ms. Caching per-point search spaces across requests would remove the 1,000 searches that a 1 × 1,000 request spends its 10 ms on.
  • Compression for thin clients. Byte-shuffled zstd shrinks the body 2.2× for ~5 ms of CPU — pointless on a 5 Gbit/s in-VPC link, useful below ~2 Gbit/s.
  • Faster cold start and a second host for zero-downtime dataset updates.
